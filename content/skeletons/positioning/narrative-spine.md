# CMO keynote - narrative spine (v1, 26 Aug 2026)

**Deliverable:** one PowerPoint, 15 minutes.
**Speaker:** Jay, as an independent thought leader. No Progress affiliation, no product
name, no product screenshots, no pitch, no CTA.
**Room:** CMOs of Fortune 500 companies.
**Job of the talk:** they leave believing they can build the experiences their customers
and their own people actually need - and leave with specific ideas they want to try in
their own department. They should be imagining what we sell before they know it exists.

---

## Thesis

**Everything you can buy, your competitor can buy. The only experiences that
differentiate you are the ones you build - and the economics of building just changed
in your favour.**

The talk is a strategy argument about ownership and differentiation. AI is what makes
the argument newly true; it is not the subject.

---

## The arc - five movements

### 1. The sameness trap (0:00 - 3:00)

Open on the landscape: roughly 14,000 marketing tools (verify, cite, date on-slide).

You have bought hundreds of them. The uncomfortable part: so has your competitor. Same
vendors, same roadmaps, same capabilities, same "personalisation engine" behind three
different logos.

Procurement is a flattening force. When everyone buys the same capability, capability
stops being a differentiator, and the fight collapses back to budget and brand.

And you do not own any of it. You rent a roadmap. Your best ideas go into a vendor's
feature-request queue and come back in eighteen months - shipped on the same day to your
competitors.

**Land the tension: you are spending more than ever in order to become more like
everyone else.**

### 2. Why you bought instead of built - and what changed (3:00 - 6:30)

Be fair to the past. Buying was the right decision. Building software meant building the
foundation first: understanding language, searching by meaning, connecting to every
system, security, scale. That was ninety per cent of the work and none of it was
marketing. So you bought the whole thing to get the last ten per cent.

What changed is that the foundation became a commodity. The hard, expensive,
general-purpose part - the part that reads your material and answers questions from it -
is now something you rent through an interface and stand up in an afternoon.

So the ratio flipped. Building no longer means building everything. It means building
the thin layer that is uniquely yours, on a foundation you no longer have to build.

**The ninety per cent is now bought. The ten per cent that is actually you is now
buildable.**

### 3. The two audiences nobody sells you (6:30 - 10:00)

Two experiences vendors will never build well, because they do not know your business,
your material, or your people.

**Your customers.** Not a chatbot bolted to the corner of a page. Your brand answering -
in your voice, from your own material - the questions your customers actually ask, on
the surfaces where they ask them.

**Your own people.** The experience no vendor sells, because there is no market category
for it. Your organisation knows an enormous amount and cannot reach it. What did we
claim, what is approved, what did that campaign do, what does legal allow, what have we
already made. Your marketers spend their days doing archaeology on your own knowledge.

**The symmetry to land: the two audiences you understand better than any vendor are the
two you have never been able to build for.**

### 4. What becomes possible (10:00 - 13:00)

The inspiration movement. Concrete, universally recognisable to any large-company CMO,
each phrased as an experience rather than a technology, each small enough to start on
Monday:

- A site experience that answers, instead of listing pages.
- A brand brain that every region, agency and team asks, instead of emailing you.
- Campaign memory - everything you have run, claimed and learned, answerable in seconds.
- Support that resolves rather than deflects.
- Catalogue understanding - recommendations that know what the things actually are.
- Market and competitor reading at a volume no team can read.

Then the discipline beat, which is what earns the room's trust: this is not a licence to
build everything. Building the foundation yourself is still a mistake. The skill is
knowing which ten per cent is yours.

Then the takeaway rubric - four questions to hold any of it to:

1. Is it grounded in *our* material, or in the open internet?
2. Can it show its work - cite the source - so it is auditable and brand-safe?
3. Can we tell whether an answer was good, one answer at a time?
4. Does it run where our data is allowed to live?

(This is the category-shaping move. It is a genuine buyer's rubric, it is the right
advice, and it is also the criteria we win on - stated without naming anything.)

### 5. The close (13:00 - 15:00)

Not a call to action. A challenge.

Pick one experience. One your customers need, or one your own people need, that no
vendor sells you. Build that one.

**Closing line: "You have spent a decade buying tools that made you the same as everyone
else. Spend the next year building three things that make you different."**

---

## Deliberate choices

- **No CTA, no QR, no landing page.** The idea is the payload. A CTA would betray the
  frame and the room would feel it.
- **Bought-versus-built, not "AI".** Avoids AI-keynote fatigue. This is a strategy talk
  that happens to be enabled by AI, which is why it can carry a room of people who have
  already sat through four AI keynotes that day.
- **The rubric does the work.** No product is named, but the audience leaves holding the
  evaluation criteria.
- **Custom-designed deck, not the corporate template** - the FY26 template is
  unmistakably Progress and would break the frame on slide one.

## To verify before build

- The martech tool count, current year, cited on-slide, authoritative source.
- Every other statistic - authoritative sources only, dated.

# Delivery design (GM directive, 26 Aug 2026)

The GM delivers this live to a captive, respected audience. Two binding requirements:
the talk track must **sound like him**, and the whole thing must be **impactful and
simple to memorise and deliver**. Those are design constraints on the artifact, not
notes appended after it is built.

## Making it simple to memorise

The enemy is a 2,000-word script he has to hold in his head. The architecture below
means he only ever has to remember **five things**, and the slides carry the rest.

1. **Five movements, one idea each.** He memorises five ideas, not eighteen slides.
2. **One spine sentence per movement.** The single line that must land. Everything
   around it can be delivered differently every time without damaging the talk.
3. **A refrain, said three times.** "Everything you can buy, your competitor can buy."
   Opens the talk, returns at the hinge, closes it. Three placements makes it memorable
   for him to deliver and for the room to repeat afterwards - which is how a talk
   travels beyond the room.
4. **Rule of three inside each movement.** Three tools, three questions, three things to
   build. Threes are how people hold structure without notes.
5. **The slide IS the cue card.** This is the key move: an impactful keynote slide and a
   memory aid are the same artifact when designed properly. Each slide carries the
   anchor idea in large type, so if he loses his place he simply reads what the room is
   already looking at and the next sentence arrives on its own. He can never be stranded.
6. **One visual he can see in his head.** The 90/10 flip becomes a single picture that
   holds movement 2 together in his memory and the room's.
7. **Scripted transitions.** The hardest part of delivering from memory is the joins.
   Every movement gets a written one-line bridge into the next, because a fumbled join
   is what makes a speaker reach for notes.

## Slide design principles

- Very few words. Large type. One idea per slide. No bullet lists anywhere.
- A number that matters gets a whole slide to itself.
- Roughly 18-22 slides for 15 minutes - averaging about 45 seconds, with statement
  slides held for ten and substance slides for ninety.
- Designed for a large room and a distant back row: high contrast, generous scale.
- No logos, no template chrome, no affiliation. Nothing that identifies a vendor.

## The talk track, in his voice

Source of truth is the `jay` profile in `brand/voices.json` and the fuller
`jay-sanderson-voice` reference memory. Translated from his written register to spoken
delivery:

- **Practitioner reporting back, never a pundit selling.** He has built these things.
  He says so, without naming what he built them on.
- **Hard-varied sentence length.** A long flowing sentence, then a two-word fragment
  that lands it. This is his signature and it is also, conveniently, exactly how good
  stage delivery breathes.
- **Cold open.** No throat-clearing, no "in today's fast-paced world", no thanking the
  organisers in the script.
- **One honest concession, always.** He never oversells. Movement 4's discipline beat
  carries this and it is what earns a sceptical room.
- **Plain Anglo-Saxon words**; any technical term glossed the first time it appears.
- **Concrete numbers over adjectives.**
- **Build to one reframe, then restate it tighter at the end.**
- **Dry, low-key humour** where it earns its place. Never a joke that needs to land.
- Australian spelling. Spaced hyphens, not em dashes, since this is a deck.

## Deliverable shape

One PowerPoint, delivered to the GM, in which every slide carries:
- the slide itself, built as a cue card that is also a strong keynote visual;
- **speaker notes containing the verbatim talk track** in his voice, so he can learn it;
- **an ANCHOR line at the top of each note** - the one sentence for that slide. If he
  remembers only the anchors, he can still deliver the whole talk.

That dual layer serves both delivery modes: learn it verbatim, or run on anchors and
riff. He should not have to choose in advance, and on the day he will do some of each.

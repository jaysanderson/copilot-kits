## Start with the age of the calf

The age of the affected calves narrows the cause more than any other fact. Scours in calves under 5 days old is most often caused by enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli, and at 5 to 14 days by rotavirus or coronavirus. Cryptosporidium causes scours at 7 to 21 days. Salmonella can occur at any age but is most important in calves over 10 days and in the presence of fever and blood. Coccidiosis (Eimeria) affects calves from three weeks of age and is the likely cause of scours with straining in calves at 4 to 8 weeks.

## Assess the calf

Every sick calf is scored for dehydration, suck reflex and ability to stand. Skin tent duration under 2 seconds with a strong suck reflex indicates less than 6 per cent dehydration. A tent of 2 to 6 seconds and a weak suck indicates 6 to 10 per cent. A calf that cannot stand, with cold extremities and a sunken eye, is more than 10 per cent dehydrated and is acidotic. Most deaths in scouring calves are from dehydration and acidosis, not from infection.

## Treatment

A calf with a suck reflex and a skin tent under 2 seconds is rehydrated orally with an electrolyte solution containing sodium, glucose and an alkalinising agent such as bicarbonate or acetate, at 2 litres, twice daily, in addition to its normal milk. Milk is not withdrawn. A calf that cannot stand receives intravenous isotonic fluids with bicarbonate; the volume to give in litres is body weight in kilograms, multiplied by the per cent dehydration, divided by 100, with maintenance added. Antimicrobials are reserved for calves with fever, blood in faeces or signs of septicaemia, and are not given to uncomplicated viral or cryptosporidial scours.

## Sampling

Faeces from at least 5 untreated, acutely scouring calves, taken before treatment, are collected into a clean pot, not from the ground, and chilled. One sample per pathogen is not enough. The laboratory runs a multiplex PCR for rotavirus, coronavirus and Cryptosporidium, culture for Salmonella and E. coli, and a flotation for coccidial oocysts. Cryptosporidium is confirmed by faecal PCR or by a modified acid-fast stain, and a result is reported in 1 to 2 working days. A calf that dies is submitted whole and chilled within 4 hours, with the small intestine fixed in formalin.

## Failure of passive transfer

Calves are given 3 to 4 litres of colostrum within 6 hours of birth. Serum IgG below 10 grams per litre at 24 to 48 hours of age means failure of passive transfer. Using a refractometer, a serum total protein below 5.2 grams per decilitre corresponds to failure. A herd target is that at least 90 per cent of calves have adequate transfer. Failure is the biggest risk factor for scours and septicaemia in the first month.

## Herd factors to review

Check the calving paddock: stocking density above 10 cows per hectare of calving area raises the infection pressure. Check the colostrum: cows' colostrum should have a Brix reading above 22 per cent. Check the pens: the calf-to-calf transmission is greatest when calves are moved into pens that have not been cleaned and rested for at least 14 days. Check the milk feeding: mix errors in milk powder, and cold or irregular feeding, cause nutritional scours that mimic infection.

## Prevention

Vaccinate dams against rotavirus, coronavirus and E. coli 3 to 6 weeks before calving to raise colostral antibody. Move calving cows to a clean paddock. Remove calves from the dam's pen within hours in dairy herds. Keep sick calves in an isolated hospital pen, fed and handled last by a dedicated worker.

## Zoonotic risk

Cryptosporidium and Salmonella are both zoonotic. Handlers wash hands with soap and water, not hand rub, because alcohol does not kill Cryptosporidium oocysts. Students do not handle scouring calves without gloves, and clothes worn for the scours pen are laundered the same day.

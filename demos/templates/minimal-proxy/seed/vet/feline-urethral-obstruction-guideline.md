## What to do with a blocked cat

A blocked male cat is managed in a fixed order: assess for life-threatening hyperkalaemia, correct it, relieve the obstruction under sedation or anaesthesia, place an indwelling catheter, give fluids, and monitor for post-obstructive diuresis. The first priority is the heart, not the bladder.

## Presentation and assessment

Cats present straining in the litter tray, vocalising, licking at the penis, and often vomiting and depressed. The bladder is large, firm and painful on gentle palpation, and should not be squeezed. Severe cases are bradycardic, hypothermic and collapsed. Blood is taken at catheter placement for potassium, ionised calcium, urea, creatinine, packed cell volume and venous blood gas. An ECG is run at once. Bradycardia, a flattened or absent P wave, a spiked T wave and a wide QRS are signs of hyperkalaemia.

## Treating hyperkalaemia first

Serum potassium above 8 millimoles per litre, or bradycardia with ECG changes, is treated before catheterisation with intravenous calcium gluconate. Calcium gluconate 10 per cent is given at 0.5 millilitres per kilogram slowly over 10 minutes while watching the ECG, and the infusion is stopped if the heart rate drops further. Calcium protects the heart for 30 to 60 minutes but does not lower the potassium. Potassium is then moved into cells with regular insulin at 0.25 units per kilogram intravenously followed by 1 to 2 millilitres per kilogram of 50 per cent dextrose, or with dextrose alone. Blood glucose is checked hourly for 6 hours because hypoglycaemia can follow insulin.

## Relieving the obstruction

Cats are sedated or briefly anaesthetised, with an opioid and a short-acting induction agent chosen to suit cardiovascular status. The penis is extruded caudally, the tip is cleaned, and the urethra is flushed gently with warm sterile saline through a lubricated open-ended catheter to dislodge plugs. A soft 3.5 or 4 French indwelling catheter is then passed. If the bladder is very tense and the urethra cannot be cleared quickly, cystocentesis with a fine needle can decompress it briefly, although it carries a small risk of bladder rupture. Once the catheter is in, the bladder is emptied and lavaged until the effluent clears.

## Catheter care

The urinary catheter is left in place for 24 to 48 hours with a closed collection system. It is sutured to the prepuce, an Elizabethan collar is fitted, and the collection bag is kept below the level of the bladder. Prophylactic antibiotics are not given while the catheter is in place, since they select for resistant organisms. Urine is cultured from a cystocentesis sample, or from the catheter tip, if the cat is later symptomatic or if the catheter was in place beyond 72 hours. The catheter is removed when urine is pink or clear and the output is stable, and the cat is observed for 6 to 12 hours to confirm normal voiding.

## Fluids and post-obstructive diuresis

Intravenous isotonic crystalloid is started at a shock rate of 10 to 20 millilitres per kilogram over 15 to 30 minutes if the cat is hypovolaemic, then moved to a rate of 2 to 3 times maintenance. Post-obstructive diuresis is expected, and urine output above 2 millilitres per kilogram per hour is matched with replacement fluids. Potassium and electrolytes are rechecked at 4, 12 and 24 hours. A cat producing less than 1 millilitre per kilogram per hour after relief of the obstruction is oliguric and needs renal investigation. Analgesia continues with buprenorphine, and a muscle relaxant such as prazosin is used for urethral spasm after catheter removal.

## Discharge criteria

A cat is discharged when it passes a good stream of urine unaided, is eating, is bright, has normalised creatinine and potassium, and has been observed for at least 12 hours after catheter removal. Most cats are in hospital for 2 to 3 days.

## Preventing reblockage

Around one in four cats reblock within 6 months. Owners are told to change the cat to a urinary diet for life after a first obstruction, with wet food preferred, to increase water intake with fountains and multiple bowls, to reduce stress and keep litter trays clean, and to manage obesity. Recurrent obstruction is considered for perineal urethrostomy, which is a salvage procedure after repeated blockage rather than a first-line choice, and which carries a risk of stricture and urinary tract infection.

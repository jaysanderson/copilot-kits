## What to do with a suspected parvovirus case

A puppy with vomiting and bloody diarrhoea is treated as parvovirus until tested. Suspect cases are taken directly to the isolation ward and never enter the general waiting area or ward. The sequence is: isolate, test, correct dehydration and electrolytes, control vomiting and pain, feed early, and keep in isolation until the puppy is no longer shedding.

## Arrival and isolation

Owners telephone from the car park, and staff collect the puppy in a covered carrier or on a towel. The puppy is carried straight to isolation. Staff wear gowns, gloves, overshoes and a mask, change them when leaving, and wash hands with soap before disinfecting. Isolation uses dedicated equipment, including stethoscope, thermometer, bowls and catheter trays. Isolation staff do not handle other patients on the same shift where possible. The ward is cleaned twice daily, and waste is double-bagged.

## Diagnosis

Faecal antigen testing is run on a rectal swab or faecal sample. A positive result confirms the diagnosis, but a negative result in the first 3 days of illness does not exclude it, so a negative in a puppy with typical signs is repeated or confirmed by faecal PCR. A blood count typically shows a leukopenia with a white cell count below 3 x 10^9 per litre, and a falling count indicates a worse outlook. Blood is also taken for packed cell volume, total solids, glucose, electrolytes and albumin. Hypoglycaemia is common in small puppies and is corrected with dextrose.

## Fluid and supportive treatment

Treatment is supportive, with intravenous crystalloids, antiemetics, analgesia and early enteral nutrition. A balanced crystalloid is given to correct dehydration over 4 to 6 hours, plus maintenance, plus ongoing losses measured by weighing bedding. Potassium chloride is added to fluids according to serum potassium, and dextrose is added at 2.5 per cent for hypoglycaemic puppies. Maropitant at 1 milligram per kilogram subcutaneously once daily controls vomiting, with ondansetron added if vomiting continues. Opioid analgesia such as buprenorphine is given for abdominal pain. Colloids or plasma are used if albumin falls below 20 grams per litre.

## Antibiotics

Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, usually ampicillin with a fluoroquinolone or an aminoglycoside after rehydration, are given to puppies with leukopenia, fever or evidence of sepsis, since bacterial translocation through damaged intestine is the main cause of death. Antibiotics are not used in mild cases that are eating.

## Early enteral nutrition

Feeding starts within 12 to 24 hours of admission once vomiting is controlled. A liquid or highly digestible diet is offered in small volumes every 2 to 4 hours, through a nasoesophageal tube if the puppy will not eat. Early feeding reduces time in hospital and supports gut recovery.

## Monitoring

Weight, temperature, heart rate, hydration, vomiting and faecal output are recorded 4 times daily. Packed cell volume, total solids and glucose are rechecked twice daily until stable. Deterioration is signalled by a falling white cell count, a rising temperature, hypotension, or a drop in albumin, and these puppies are moved to intensive nursing.

## Duration of isolation and discharge

Puppies are kept in isolation until at least 48 hours after the last vomiting and diarrhoea, and are discharged only when eating and gaining weight. Most survivors are discharged 4 to 7 days after admission. Survival with hospital treatment is around 85 to 90 per cent, and it is far lower without intravenous fluids. Shedding continues for 1 to 2 weeks after recovery, so owners keep the puppy away from other dogs for 2 weeks after discharge.

## Disinfection and owner advice

The virus survives in the environment for months and is inactivated by a 1 in 32 dilution of household bleach with 10 minutes contact time, applied to hard surfaces after cleaning off organic matter. Other dogs in the household are checked for vaccination status. Owners are told that puppies are vaccinated at 6 to 8 weeks, 10 to 12 weeks and 14 to 16 weeks, and are not taken to public places until 1 week after the final vaccination.

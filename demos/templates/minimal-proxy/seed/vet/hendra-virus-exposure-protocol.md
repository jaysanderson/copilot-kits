# Hendra Virus Exposure Protocol for Equine Referrals

## Scope

This protocol applies to every horse presented to the teaching hospital with acute febrile illness accompanied by neurological or respiratory signs, where Hendra virus infection has not been excluded by laboratory testing. It applies regardless of the referring practice's own assessment. It also governs what happens to any person who has contact with body fluids from such a horse, and what happens to the bay, the equipment and the animal afterwards.

## Triage on arrival

Vaccination status is established before the animal leaves the float. A current vaccination record reduces but does not eliminate the index of suspicion, and the absence of a record is treated as unvaccinated. Where status cannot be established within ten minutes, the case proceeds on the unvaccinated pathway.

Suspect cases are held in the external isolation bay. The bay is used for no other purpose while a suspect case occupies it, and the adjacent wash area is closed. A suspect case is not admitted to the general equine ward before laboratory exclusion.

## Personal protective equipment

Full respiratory protection, eye protection, impermeable gown and double gloves are worn by every person entering the bay, including the referring veterinarian. Students do not enter a suspect case bay under any circumstances, and observation is by video link from the adjoining room.

## Sampling

Samples are taken in an order that minimises aerosol generation during restraint. EDTA blood is drawn first. A nasal swab follows only if the animal is adequately restrained and sedation has taken effect. Urine is collected passively where possible rather than by catheterisation. Every sample is double-bagged at the point of collection and labelled before it leaves the bay.

## What to do when a person is exposed

An exposure is any splash of nasal discharge, saliva, urine, blood or foetal fluid onto the eyes, nose or mouth (a mucosal exposure), any needlestick, cut or bite through the skin (a percutaneous exposure), or any contact with these fluids on broken skin. Entering the bay without full protective equipment while the horse is shedding is also recorded as an exposure even when no splash is seen.

The exposed person acts in this order.

1. They stop work and leave the bay through the doffing area.
2. They wash the affected skin for fifteen minutes with soap and running water, and irrigate eyes, nose or mouth with clean water or saline for fifteen minutes. Percutaneous wounds are encouraged to bleed gently and are not scrubbed.
3. They tell the senior clinician in charge, who takes over supervision of the bay.

The senior clinician then notifies in a fixed sequence. First, the attending clinician telephones the state chief veterinary officer before the animal is sampled, so that a laboratory submission number is issued and the official investigation begins. Second, the hospital biosecurity officer is told within the hour. Third, the public health unit on-call physician is telephoned, and the exposed person is referred to the occupational health clinic. Fourth, the director of the teaching hospital is informed by the end of the shift.

## Recording the exposure

The hospital biosecurity officer records the exposure within the same shift on the exposure register. The record states the name of the person, the time and body site of the exposure, the type of fluid, the protective equipment worn, the vaccination status of the horse and the first-aid actions taken. Every other person who entered the bay in the preceding seventy-two hours is listed as a contact, and the register entry is closed only when the public health unit issues its written clearance.

## Follow-up serology and exclusion from work

The exposed person has baseline serum collected on the day of exposure, and a convalescent sample is taken twenty-one days later, with a further sample at six weeks if the horse is confirmed positive. The public health unit decides the monitoring schedule and the occupational health clinic carries it out.

An exposed person is excluded from clinical work and from all contact with horses until the public health unit clears them. If the horse is excluded by laboratory testing, clearance is usually issued within two working days. If the horse is confirmed positive, the exclusion continues for the monitoring period set by the public health unit, and the person is checked daily for fever, headache, cough or neurological signs. Anyone who develops these signs telephones the public health unit rather than attending the hospital.

## Decontamination of the bay and equipment

The bay stays closed while the case occupies it and until the laboratory has reported. If the horse is confirmed positive, disinfection follows the official quarantine direction. All disposable equipment is double-bagged and incinerated. Reusable equipment is washed with detergent, then soaked in a one per cent sodium hypochlorite solution for ten minutes. Bedding and manure stay in the bay until the official direction allows removal. Staff wearing full protective equipment wash the bay surfaces with detergent before the disinfectant is applied, and the bay is left empty for a minimum of twenty-eight days after a positive case.

## Laboratory exclusion pathway for the animal

Samples go by courier on the day of collection to the state reference laboratory under the submission number from the chief veterinary officer. Polymerase chain reaction results are normally available within twenty-four hours. A horse is excluded only after two negative results on samples taken twenty-four hours apart, together with a clinical assessment that the signs have another diagnosis. Until exclusion, the animal stays in the isolation bay. If a horse deteriorates, euthanasia is performed in the bay, and necropsy happens only under official direction.

## Refusal of admission

Where isolation capacity is unavailable, the case is refused and the referring practice is advised to hold the animal on its own premises with the same precautions. This is a safety decision and is not subject to clinical override.

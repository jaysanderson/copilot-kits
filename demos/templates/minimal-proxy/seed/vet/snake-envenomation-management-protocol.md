## What to do when a snake bite is suspected

Treat any collapsed animal, with or without a witnessed bite, as envenomated until tests show otherwise. The sequence is: stabilise, place an intravenous catheter, take blood for coagulation testing, give antivenom, then monitor. Antivenom is not delayed for laboratory results when the history and signs are typical.

## First-aid advice given by telephone

Owners are told to keep the animal still, carry rather than walk it, and apply a pressure bandage to the bitten limb if the bite site is on a limb. Pressure immobilisation is applied by the owner or first responder and removed only once antivenom is ready. Owners are told not to wash the wound, cut it, suck it, or apply a tourniquet, and not to try to catch or kill the snake. The practice is warned that the animal is coming.

## Clinical signs

Early signs include sudden weakness followed by collapse, then apparent recovery, then repeated collapse, vomiting, salivation and dilated pupils. Later signs include progressive flaccid paralysis, urine that is dark brown from myoglobin or haemoglobin, and bleeding from catheter sites. Cats more often present with paralysis and respiratory failure, and dogs more often with collapse and coagulopathy. Often no bite wound can be found, and absence of a wound does not exclude envenomation.

## Initial tests

A baseline set is taken at catheter placement: a 20-minute whole blood clotting time in a plain glass tube, packed cell volume, total solids, glucose, lactate, creatinine, creatine kinase and a urine sample. A 20-minute whole blood clotting time is run on arrival and repeated 6 hours after antivenom. A clot that has not formed at 20 minutes indicates venom-induced consumptive coagulopathy. Creatine kinase above 1000 units per litre indicates myotoxicity. Where available, a venom detection kit is run on urine or a bite swab to inform choice of antivenom, but it never delays treatment.

## Antivenom

Antivenom is chosen by the venom detection result where it is available, and a polyvalent product is used where the snake is unidentified or the kit is unavailable. Antivenom is given as a single vial diluted 1 to 10 in isotonic saline and infused over 20 to 30 minutes, with a second vial only if clinical signs or coagulopathy persist at 6 hours. Adrenaline is drawn up at the bedside before the infusion starts. Reactions are rare in animals but are treated immediately by stopping the infusion, giving adrenaline and fluids, and resuming at half rate once stable. Early antivenom is the single most effective intervention, and benefit declines with each hour of delay.

## Supportive care

Intravenous crystalloids are run at maintenance rate, adjusted if there are signs of renal injury or myoglobinuria. Fresh frozen plasma is given at 10 millilitres per kilogram only for active bleeding or a coagulopathy that persists after antivenom, since plasma without antivenom just supplies new substrate for the venom. Corticosteroids are not used. Sedation is minimised, and respiratory function is monitored hourly for the first 6 hours. Oxygen is given for hypoxaemia, and animals with respiratory failure are mechanically ventilated. Vomiting is treated with maropitant, and analgesia uses a low dose of opioid, because paralysed animals have reduced ventilation.

## Monitoring and escalation

The clotting time is repeated 6 hours after antivenom. A normal result at that point, with improving strength, indicates successful neutralisation. A persistent clotting abnormality at 6 hours triggers the second vial. Intensive care is indicated for respiratory failure, a creatine kinase over 5000 units per litre, anuria or oliguria, or any bleeding. Urine output is measured through a closed collection system, with a target of at least 1 millilitre per kilogram per hour.

## Duration of observation and discharge

Animals are monitored for at least 24 hours after antivenom, and for 48 hours if neurological signs were present. An animal is discharged when it can walk and swallow normally, has a normal clotting time, has a normal urine colour, and has eaten. Owners are told to return the animal immediately if weakness, dark urine or bleeding recurs, and to keep the animal confined and calm at home for 3 days.

## Prognosis and communication

With early antivenom, most dogs survive. Cats with paralysis and respiratory failure have a lower survival rate, and recovery of muscle strength in a cat may take several days. Owners are given an estimate of cost, which in a hospital stay of 24 to 48 hours is dominated by antivenom, and are told that a second bite later in life is a risk, as are exposure to snake-prone areas in the warm months.

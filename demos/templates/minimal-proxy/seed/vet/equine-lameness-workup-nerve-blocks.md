## Why the order matters

A lameness examination at the teaching hospital follows a fixed order so that two clinicians reach the same answer from the same horse. The sequence is history, static examination, palpation, flexion tests, observed gait on a hard surface and in a circle, diagnostic analgesia, and only then imaging. Skipping to imaging first produces films of abnormal but irrelevant structures.

## Grading and recording

Lameness is graded zero to five on the lameness scale used by the hospital. Grade 0 is sound. Grade 1 is difficult to see and inconsistent. Grade 2 is seen consistently at the trot on a straight line. Grade 3 is obvious at the trot. Grade 4 is obvious at the walk. Grade 5 is minimal weight bearing or non-weight bearing. The grade is recorded on the hospital record for each limb before any block is performed, and again after each block. Video of the horse trotting on a straight line and in both directions on the lunge is taken before and after every block.

## Flexion tests and hoof testers

Distal limb flexion tests are held for sixty seconds and the horse is trotted off immediately. A test is positive if the lameness increases by at least one grade for the first three to five strides. Hoof testers are applied systematically around the sole, and a withdrawal response over the frog or toe is recorded. Flexion tests are not diagnostic by themselves, and a positive flexion is followed by a nerve block, not treatment.

## Order of diagnostic nerve blocks

Blocks proceed from distal to proximal so that each result narrows the region. The standard order for a forelimb is as follows.

1. Palmar digital nerve block, which desensitises the heel region and the navicular area. Anaesthetic volume is 2 ml per nerve of mepivacaine 2 per cent.
2. Abaxial sesamoid block, 4 to 5 ml per side, desensitising the fetlock joint and below.
3. Low four-point block, 3 to 4 ml at each site, for the region below the carpus.
4. High palmar block and, where the lameness persists, the median and ulnar nerve blocks.

For the hindlimb, the order is the same distal sequence, followed by a tibial and peroneal block, and then local infiltration for the proximal suspensory ligament only when the lower limb has been excluded. Intra-articular analgesia of the distal interphalangeal joint, the fetlock and the carpus is performed after perineural blocks have localised the area.

## Reading a result

A block is read as positive when lameness improves by at least 70 per cent, assessed at ten minutes after injection. The horse is reassessed again at thirty minutes because the effect of mepivacaine lasts ninety minutes and a late improvement suggests spread to a more proximal structure. A partial improvement of 30 to 70 per cent is called equivocal, and the next block in the sequence is performed to confirm. A horse that is not improved by the abaxial sesamoid block but is improved by the low four-point block localises to the region between the fetlock and the carpus.

A block is never performed on a horse at grade 5 or in any case where a fracture is suspected, because loading a fractured limb after analgesia can cause catastrophic failure. These horses go straight to radiography with the limb supported.

## Aseptic technique

Intra-articular and intrathecal injections use full aseptic preparation with a five-minute chlorhexidine scrub, an alcohol rinse and sterile gloves. Perineural blocks use a clipped site and a single-use needle. Septic arthritis is the principal avoidable complication, and an infected joint is a surgical emergency. Any horse with new heat, swelling or lameness at 24 hours after a joint block is returned for assessment the same day.

## When to request imaging

Radiographs are requested only after the lameness has been localised to a region by blocking. The ordering clinician states the blocked region and the grade on the imaging request. Ultrasound is requested for suspected soft tissue injury in the pastern, suspensory ligament and flexor tendons. Nuclear scintigraphy or magnetic resonance imaging is reserved for cases where a block has localised a problem but radiographs and ultrasound are unremarkable. The imaging request form asks for the lameness grade, the block results and the volume of anaesthetic used.

## Discharge and rest

After blocks, the horse is rested in a box for the remainder of the day. No anti-inflammatory drugs are given before the end of the examination, because they confound the result. The owner is told that the horse may be sore the next day and that a lameness that increases is a reason to telephone the hospital.

## Case example of reading the sequence

A horse is graded 3 on the left fore at the trot. The palmar digital block removes less than 30 per cent of the lameness. The abaxial sesamoid block removes about 80 per cent at ten minutes. The lameness is therefore localised between the heel region and the fetlock joint, and radiographs of the foot and fetlock are requested, with ultrasound of the digital flexor tendons in the pastern. The same horse is rechecked at 30 minutes to confirm the block is wearing off in the expected way before any treatment decision is made.

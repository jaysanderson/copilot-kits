## Background

Cranial cruciate ligament disease remains the most common orthopaedic presentation in the small animal surgical unit. This review covers twelve months of repairs with the aim of informing student teaching rather than establishing a surgical preference.

## Diagnosis before surgery

The diagnosis rests on the cranial drawer test and the tibial compression test, performed awake and repeated under sedation if the dog resists. Stifle effusion and a medial buttress are supportive. Radiographs are taken to show joint effusion, rule out other causes of lameness and allow the tibial plateau angle to be measured for surgical planning. Partial tears are suspected when the drawer test is negative but effusion and a pivot-type lameness are present, and these are re-examined under anaesthesia before the decision is made.

## Which technique is chosen

Technique was selected by bodyweight band and activity expectation rather than by surgeon preference alone. Tibial plateau levelling osteotomy was chosen for most large-breed dogs, defined as dogs of 15 kilograms and over, and for working animals of any size. Extracapsular stabilisation was used predominantly below the small-breed weight threshold of 15 kilograms, where the review found comparable outcomes at lower cost and shorter anaesthetic time. A tibial plateau angle above 30 degrees favoured osteotomy in the unit regardless of weight, because steep plateaus were judged to load the extracapsular repair excessively.

## Meniscal assessment

The medial meniscus is assessed at every surgery, either by arthroscopy or a small arthrotomy. A torn meniscus is treated by partial meniscectomy of the damaged portion only. Meniscal injury identified after the initial procedure, recorded as a sudden worsening of lameness with a palpable click between 6 weeks and 6 months post-operatively, was recorded in both groups and is managed with a second arthroscopy.

## Complications

Surgical site infection was the most frequently recorded complication across both techniques and was concentrated in cases where post-operative confinement was poorly observed at home. Infections presented as discharge, swelling and licking at the incision between day 5 and day 14, and were managed with culture, antibiotics and, for implant-associated infection, removal of the implant once the osteotomy had healed. Implant loosening was recorded only in the osteotomy group. Fibular fracture and patellar tendon inflammation were recorded rarely after osteotomy, and suture breakdown with a lateral instability recurrence was the rare extracapsular failure.

## Post-operative care

Analgesia is multimodal: an opioid in hospital, followed by a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug at home for 7 to 10 days. Cold compresses are applied to the stifle for 10 minutes three times daily for the first 3 days. An Elizabethan collar is worn until suture removal at 10 to 14 days. Confinement is strict for 8 weeks after surgery, which means crate rest or a small room, lead-only toileting for 5 minutes at a time, no stairs and no slippery floors. Controlled lead walks begin at 5 minutes twice daily from week 2 for osteotomy cases and from week 3 for extracapsular cases, with an increase of 5 minutes per week. A recheck with radiographs is done at 8 weeks. Off-lead exercise begins only if the radiographs show bone healing in osteotomy cases and the limb is bearing weight comfortably.

## Return to function

Return to unrestricted activity was recorded at the final recheck, at around 12 to 16 weeks for most dogs. The review notes that owner compliance with the confinement schedule was the strongest single predictor of an uncomplicated recovery, ahead of technique, bodyweight or age. Dogs with weight control and physiotherapy returned to function earlier. About half of dogs with one ruptured ligament later showed signs in the opposite stifle, and owners are told this at the first consultation.

## What owners are told

Owners are told that surgery stabilises the joint but does not remove arthritis, that the recovery takes 12 to 16 weeks, that the first 8 weeks of confinement matter most, that body condition is kept lean, and that the opposite stifle may follow. Conservative management is offered only to small, lightly active dogs, and is expected to take months to settle.

## Teaching implications

Students should not read this review as a ranking of techniques. The useful lesson is that selection criteria and post-operative management explain more of the outcome variation than the choice between the two procedures.

## Case definition and first response

A horse with fever above 38.5 degrees Celsius and either submandibular lymph node swelling or purulent nasal discharge is a strangles suspect. The horse is isolated immediately, before any test result, and every other horse that has shared airspace, water or handlers is recorded as a contact. Contacts have their temperatures taken twice daily. A rise above 38.5 degrees Celsius moves a contact to the suspect group.

## Zoning the premises

The property is divided into three zones. The red zone holds clinical cases and suspects. The amber zone holds contacts that are afebrile but have been exposed. The green zone holds horses with no exposure. Each zone has its own dedicated handler, its own set of buckets, brushes and tack, and its own boots and coveralls. A person who works across zones starts in green, moves to amber and ends in red, and does not go back. Footbaths with a quaternary ammonium disinfectant at the label concentration are placed at every zone boundary and changed daily because organic matter inactivates them.

## Testing

Testing combines polymerase chain reaction and culture. For a horse with discharge or abscess material, the sample is a swab or aspirate taken from the abscess. For a horse without discharge, a nasopharyngeal swab is taken. A single negative swab does not exclude infection. Horses with positive results are not moved. Guttural pouch lavage through an endoscope is performed on every horse that has recovered, at least four weeks after the signs resolved, because up to 10 per cent of recovered horses in this synthetic scenario become silent carriers with infected material in the guttural pouch. Lavage is polymerase chain reaction tested, and a positive horse is endoscopically examined for chondroids and treated.

## Treatment

Most horses with lymph node abscessation recover without antibiotics, and antibiotics are not given routinely, because they can prolong the course by delaying abscess maturation and make a horse an unreliable immunity candidate. Hot compresses are applied to ripening abscesses and abscesses are lanced only when they are mature and soft. Anti-inflammatory drugs are used for pain and fever. Penicillin is reserved for horses with severe systemic signs, such as a high fever, depression or inability to eat, and for the treatment of guttural pouch empyema together with local therapy. A horse with respiratory obstruction from swelling may need a tracheotomy.

## Quarantine duration and lifting restrictions

Quarantine for each horse continues until it has had either three negative nasopharyngeal swabs on polymerase chain reaction taken at weekly intervals, or two negative guttural pouch lavages taken at least a week apart. The premises stay under restriction for at least 28 days after the last new case. No horse moves on or off the property in this time except under the chief veterinary officer's written permission. Movement of horses to events stops for the whole property.

## Hospital procedure for a suspect admission

A suspect strangles case presented to the hospital is held in the isolation bay, not the general ward. Staff wear gown, gloves and boots, wash hands with soap and water, and apply alcohol gel. Fomites are the main route of spread, so equipment stays in the bay. Students do not handle a suspect case. The bay is cleaned of organic matter, washed with detergent, disinfected with a 1 per cent potassium peroxymonosulfate solution at a contact time of ten minutes, and left empty for seven days before the next admission.

## Vaccination and prevention

Vaccination does not prevent strangles in an outbreak and is not used as an outbreak response on infected premises in this protocol. Prevention rests on quarantining new arrivals for 14 days, taking temperatures daily during that time and testing by nasopharyngeal swab on two occasions a week apart. Mixing of horses at events is limited and shared water troughs are avoided.

## Notification and communication

Strangles is not a notifiable disease in this scenario, but the hospital biosecurity officer notifies the district veterinarian and the owners of all contact horses on the same day that a suspect case is confirmed. The attending clinician gives the owner a written plan, including the quarantine period, the testing schedule and the disinfectant concentrations. Owners are told that blame for an outbreak is not the point, and that rapid isolation determines the number of horses infected.

## Records

The biosecurity officer maintains a line list of every horse showing the date of first fever, the date of first positive test, the zone and the outcome. The list is updated daily and is the document used to lift restrictions.

## Communication with the owner of a recovered horse

A recovered horse is not released simply because the abscess has drained. The owner is told that the horse remains a risk until guttural pouch testing is negative, that the horse should not attend events for at least six weeks from the last clinical sign, and that a horse found to carry the organism is treated with local gelatin-based penicillin therapy after chondroids are removed endoscopically. The owner signs the release form once the line list shows the horse has met the testing criteria.

## When to suspect a deficiency

Suspect a trace element deficiency when a mob shows poor growth, ill thrift and low weaning weights on a soil type known for the problem, with no heavy worm burden. Much of the south-west of Western Australia has sandy, leached soils low in selenium, cobalt and copper. White muscle disease in lambs under 3 months suggests selenium, ill thrift with watery eyes in weaners suggests cobalt, and swayback in lambs from copper-deficient ewes suggests copper.

## Sample a group, not a single animal

Samples from 8 to 10 animals, not a single animal, are needed to represent a mob. Individual results vary widely, and a single animal can be normal or low by chance. Pick animals representative of the problem, not the sickest, and sample at least 6 of the same age group. Record the paddock, soil type and the date of last supplementation.

## Selenium

Selenium status is assessed from whole blood glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) in a heparin tube. Activity below 60 units per gram haemoglobin indicates deficiency, 60 to 100 is marginal and above 100 is adequate. A lower level in lambs than in ewes shows poor transfer. Selenium is also measured in liver, where a concentration below 0.5 micromoles per kilogram wet weight is deficient. Blood must reach the laboratory within 3 days, chilled, and not frozen.

## Cobalt

Cobalt deficiency is diagnosed from serum vitamin B12, with sheep below 300 picomoles per litre considered deficient, 300 to 600 marginal and above 600 adequate. Liver B12 below 100 nanomoles per kilogram wet weight in lambs is deficient. Serum B12 reflects recent intake, so animals dosed within 6 weeks give misleading results. Serum is separated and frozen if transport takes more than 48 hours. Cattle thresholds are lower, and serum B12 below 200 picomoles per litre indicates deficiency.

## Copper

Liver copper below 20 micromoles per kilogram wet weight indicates deficiency in cattle, and above 3000 in sheep indicates a toxic risk. Plasma copper below 4 micromoles per litre suggests deficiency in cattle, but it is a less reliable test because it changes with stress and infection. Copper is an interaction problem: molybdenum and sulphur in pasture bind copper, so a herd can be deficient on pasture with apparently adequate copper. Sheep are very sensitive to copper excess, and supplementation to sheep that are not tested is a common cause of toxicity.

## Iodine

Iodine deficiency causes goitre in newborn lambs and calves and stillbirths. It is assessed by measuring thyroid weight in dead newborns, where a combined thyroid weight above 14 grams per kilogram body weight suggests deficiency, and by serum or milk iodine. Brassica crops cause secondary deficiency.

## Interpreting the results

Compare each result with the reference value for the species and the age. Results in the marginal range are interpreted with the clinical picture: marginal selenium in a flock with no white muscle disease is monitored, not treated. A response trial is the most reliable proof: treat half the mob and leave half untreated, and weigh both groups after 8 weeks. A live weight gain of at least 1.5 kilograms in the treated group confirms the deficiency in practice.

## Supplementation

Selenium is supplemented as a pelleted slow-release bolus, a drench, or a top-dressed fertiliser at 10 grams per hectare of selenium as selenate. Cobalt is given as a vitamin B12 injection of 1 milligram per lamb every 4 weeks, or as a cobalt bullet. Copper is given only after liver copper confirms deficiency. Iodine is supplemented by injection of iodised oil in late pregnancy.

## Toxicity limits

Selenium has a narrow margin, so a drench dose of 0.1 milligrams per kilogram body weight is the upper limit. A mob that has received supplement is retested after 3 months, and further supplementation depends on the result, not on routine.

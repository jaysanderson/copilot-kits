## Scope

This protocol covers horses and ponies admitted with acute laminitis, whether it is a developmental case following colic, grain overload or systemic illness, or an acute episode in an endocrine patient. The first 72 hours decide whether the horse's foot stays stable, and the steps below are carried out in this order.

## Recognition and pain scoring

The signs are a reluctance to walk, a shifting of weight from foot to foot, a bounding digital pulse, and heat in the hoof wall. The horse stands with its forelimbs forward and the hindlimbs under the body. Pain is scored on the Obel scale. Grade 1 means the horse is lame only when turned. Grade 2 means it walks stiffly but lifts a foot without difficulty. Grade 3 means it resists having a foot lifted and moves reluctantly. Grade 4 means the horse refuses to move, or moves only if forced. The score is recorded at admission and every six hours.

## Cryotherapy

Cryotherapy is the single intervention that reduces the severity of laminitis in the developmental phase. Both forelimbs, and the hindlimbs if affected, are immersed to above the fetlock in a mixture of ice and water, held at about 2 to 5 degrees Celsius, using purpose-made boots or tubs. Ice is topped up every hour and the temperature is checked. Cryotherapy continues for 48 hours in a developmental case and is stopped sooner only if the horse is distressed by the cold. Cryotherapy is not used once lameness has appeared in a horse that has already shown the signs, because by then the benefit is lost and the horse's discomfort is greater.

## Analgesia

Pain control is staged. Flunixin meglumine at 1.1 mg per kg intravenously twice daily is given for the first 48 hours, with an upper limit of five days. Phenylbutazone is an alternative at 2.2 mg per kg twice daily by mouth. If pain stays at grade 3 or above after 12 hours, a constant rate infusion of lignocaine or a fentanyl patch is added. Opioid use is documented because of its effect on gut motility. Gastric protection with omeprazole at 4 mg per kg orally once daily accompanies every course of non-steroidal drug.

## Foot support

The horse is kept on deep sand or a thick shavings bed to a depth of at least 15 cm in a box. Frog and sole support is applied within the first 24 hours using a firm foam pad, a polystyrene support or a purpose-made support boot. Support is placed under the frog and the back half of the foot. Support is never placed under the toe. Trimming is conservative during the acute phase. A farrier does not shorten the toe or lower the heel until the radiographs have been reviewed, and the farrier works to the clinician's written trim plan.

## Radiographs and prognosis

Lateral radiographs of the affected feet are taken after pain is controlled, with the horse weight-bearing on blocks. The measurements are the dorsal wall thickness, the distance between the coronary band and the extensor process, and the rotation angle of the distal phalanx. Rotation greater than 11.5 degrees is a poor prognostic sign. Sinking of the distal phalanx, shown by a reduced coronary band to extensor process distance, is more serious than rotation alone. Radiographs are repeated at seven and then 28 days to monitor progression.

## Endocrine investigation

Endocrine testing is done once pain is controlled and not at admission, because stress, pain and drugs raise insulin and cortisol and give false positives. The hospital measures basal insulin and adrenocorticotrophic hormone, and for a suspected insulin dysregulation case uses an oral sugar test performed at least two weeks after the acute phase. Horses diagnosed with pituitary pars intermedia dysfunction are treated with pergolide at 2 micrograms per kg once daily. Insulin-dysregulated horses are moved to a low-starch diet with hay below 10 per cent non-structural carbohydrate.

## Diet and exercise during recovery

All grain and lush pasture are withdrawn on admission. The horse is fed grass hay soaked for 30 minutes to reduce sugar, at 1.5 per cent of body weight per day, and a vitamin and mineral balancer. Box rest is maintained until the pain score is 1 or less and the radiographs are stable, which is usually six to eight weeks. Walking begins in-hand on soft ground for five minutes and builds by five minutes a week.

## Euthanasia review

A horse that remains at grade 4 after 72 hours of correctly delivered analgesia, or whose radiographs show sinking, penetration of the sole or loss of the hoof capsule, is referred to a euthanasia review. The review involves the senior clinician, a second clinician and the owner. Welfare is the deciding factor, and a decision to euthanase is recorded with the pain score history.

## Discharge

The owner receives a written feeding plan, a farrier plan, and a schedule of radiographic rechecks at 28 days and three months. Owners are told that recurrence is common and that the weight, diet and endocrine status of the horse decide the long-term outcome.

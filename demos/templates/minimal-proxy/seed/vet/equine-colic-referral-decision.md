## The shape of the decision

Equine colic referral is a decision made under time pressure with incomplete information, usually by a practitioner alone in a paddock. This guidance is written for that setting rather than for the hospital. It answers one question: when does a colic case leave the property for surgical assessment?

## Treating colic in the field

Before any referral decision, the practitioner examines the horse at the property in a fixed order and records each finding with a time.

**Initial assessment.** Record heart rate, respiratory rate, rectal temperature, mucous membrane colour, capillary refill time, gut sounds in all four quadrants, digital pulses, the degree of pain (quiet, pawing, rolling or violent), faecal output and the time pain began. A heart rate under 40 beats per minute with mild signs supports medical treatment on the property. A heart rate above 60 beats per minute is a referral finding, as set out below.

**Analgesia.** Flunixin meglumine at 1.1 mg per kg intravenously is the first-line drug. Xylazine at 0.3 to 0.5 mg per kg intravenously is added for severe pain, and it is given once only, because repeated sedation hides the signs that decide the case. Butylscopolamine is used only for suspected spasmodic colic. Record the dose, the time and the horse's response.

**Nasogastric tube.** Pass a tube in every colic case with moderate to severe pain, and always before giving oral fluids or paraffin oil. Siphon the stomach and measure the reflux. Any reflux in a horse with pain is abnormal. Net reflux of more than two litres means small intestinal obstruction until proven otherwise, and the horse is referred. If there is no reflux, mineral oil at 4 to 6 litres per 500 kg may be given by tube for suspected impaction of the large colon.

**Fluids.** For a mildly dehydrated horse with a suspected impaction, give 5 to 10 litres of warm water with electrolytes by tube every hour for up to three doses, provided there is no reflux. For a horse that is dehydrated and is to travel, place an intravenous catheter and start isotonic crystalloid at 10 ml per kg per hour.

**What not to do.** Do not give oral drugs or fluids to a horse with reflux. Do not withhold analgesia to see how the pain develops, because adequate analgesia is the only way to judge whether the pain persists. Do not walk a horse for hours as treatment, and do not delay loading to wait for the owner's decision about cost. Do not give repeated doses of flunixin more often than every 12 hours.

**How long to reassess.** Reassess 30 minutes after the first dose, then at one hour and at two hours. A horse that is comfortable with a heart rate below 44 beats per minute, passing faeces and with no reflux can stay on the property under hourly observation. If pain returns within two hours, or the heart rate rises, the horse is referred.

**What travels with the horse.** The catheter stays in place, and a written record goes with the horse. It lists the times and doses of every drug, each heart rate and reflux volume, rectal findings, and the owner's contact details. The practitioner telephones the hospital before the float leaves.

## When to refer: the strongest indication

Persistent pain despite adequate analgesia is the single strongest indication for referral. Adequate means a full dose of flunixin meglumine at 1.1 mg per kg intravenously, with or without a short-acting sedative such as xylazine at 0.3 to 0.5 mg per kg. A horse that is still colicky thirty minutes after that dose, or whose pain returns within two hours, is referred. Pain that was never properly addressed because the dose was too low is a different finding, and the first step is to give the correct dose.

## Findings that trigger referral

Refer when any of the following is present after the first examination.

- Heart rate persistently above 60 beats per minute. Above 80 beats per minute the prognosis falls sharply, and the horse leaves the property without waiting for further tests.
- Net reflux of more than two litres on nasogastric intubation, or reflux that returns after the stomach is emptied.
- Distended loops of small intestine or a displaced colon on rectal examination.
- Abdominal fluid lactate above 4 mmol per litre, or peripheral blood lactate above 4 mmol per litre, or a peritoneal lactate that exceeds blood lactate by more than a factor of two.
- Dark, tacky or toxic mucous membranes with a capillary refill time over three seconds.
- Absent gut sounds in all four quadrants.
- Recurrence of pain after any initial response.

A horse that rolls violently, cannot be controlled or injures itself is referred as an emergency regardless of the other findings.

## Transport time

Transport time is factored into the decision rather than used to defer it. A three-hour float makes early referral more important, not less. The common failure is to wait for certainty that surgery is needed, by which point the transport window has closed. The working rule is that if the drive to the hospital is more than ninety minutes, the referral decision is made at the first reassessment, not the second. Surgical results are best when the horse arrives within six hours of the onset of pain.

## Rectal findings

A positive rectal finding supports referral. A negative one does not exclude it, and a practitioner who cannot safely perform the examination in the field should treat that as a missing data point rather than as a reassuring result. Small or very tense horses, ponies and miniature breeds often cannot be examined safely, and the lack of a rectal result is itself a reason to lower the threshold for referral.

## What the referring practice sends ahead

The hospital asks for the following by telephone or message before arrival: the horse's age, breed and weight, the time pain began, heart rate, respiratory rate and temperature at each check, the drugs and doses given with times, reflux volume, rectal findings, any ultrasound or lactate results, and the estimated arrival time. The hospital theatre team prepares on the basis of this information.

## Cost and consent

The owner is told at the time of referral that surgical colic carries a cost of tens of thousands of dollars in this synthetic scenario, that decisions about surgery are made on arrival, and that euthanasia on welfare grounds remains an option at every stage. Owners are not asked to commit to surgery before the horse has been examined at the hospital, so a financial worry is never a reason to delay the float.

## The asymmetry

A referral that turns out to be unnecessary costs the owner money and the practitioner some credibility. A referral that comes too late costs the horse its life. Those are not comparable costs, and the decision should not be made as though they were. When in doubt, refer.

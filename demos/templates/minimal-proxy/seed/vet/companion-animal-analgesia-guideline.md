## How analgesia is planned

Every surgical patient has an analgesia plan written before anaesthesia. The plan combines drugs that act at different points on the pain pathway, because multimodal analgesia gives better relief with lower doses of each agent. The plan covers premedication, intraoperative care, recovery and discharge.

## Assessing pain

Pain is scored at least every 4 hours with the Glasgow Composite Measure Pain Scale for dogs and the Feline Grimace Scale for cats, and analgesia is given when the score crosses the intervention threshold. For dogs, the threshold is a score of 6 out of 24, or 5 out of 20 if the dog cannot walk. For cats, a grimace score of 4 out of 10 prompts rescue analgesia. Scores are recorded on the treatment chart, and a score that does not fall within 30 minutes of an intervention is escalated to the clinician.

## Opioids

Moderate to severe pain is treated with a full mu agonist opioid such as methadone at 0.2 to 0.5 milligrams per kilogram, combined with a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug where there is no contraindication. Methadone is given intramuscularly or intravenously every 4 to 6 hours. Mild to moderate pain in cats is often treated with buprenorphine at 0.02 to 0.03 milligrams per kilogram every 6 to 8 hours, given by the buccal route. Opioids cause sedation, bradycardia and panting, and their effects are reversed with naloxone only when respiratory depression is life-threatening, because it also reverses analgesia. Fentanyl infusions at 2 to 5 micrograms per kilogram per hour are used for severe pain in intensive care.

## Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs

Meloxicam is given at 0.2 milligrams per kilogram on day 1, then 0.1 milligrams per kilogram daily in dogs, and at 0.05 milligrams per kilogram in cats for short periods. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are not given to animals that are hypovolaemic, hypotensive, azotaemic or already on a corticosteroid. They are also avoided in animals with gastrointestinal ulceration, coagulopathy or liver disease. They are given after anaesthesia, once the animal is normotensive and eating, and not before. A washout of 5 days is required when changing between a non-steroidal and a corticosteroid.

## Local anaesthesia

Local anaesthetic blocks are used for all dental, orthopaedic and body wall procedures. Bupivacaine at no more than 2 milligrams per kilogram in dogs and 1 milligram per kilogram in cats is used for infraorbital, mandibular, maxillary, epidural, sciatic-femoral and intercostal blocks. Lignocaine is used for fast-onset blocks and is not used in cats at doses above 2 milligrams per kilogram. Total dose is calculated before drawing up, and a 5 per cent margin below the limit is kept.

## Adjuncts

Ketamine at a bolus of 0.5 milligrams per kilogram, then 10 micrograms per kilogram per minute, is added in animals with severe or neuropathic pain. Gabapentin at 10 milligrams per kilogram orally twice daily is used for chronic and neuropathic pain, and as an anxiolytic in cats before travel. Paracetamol is toxic to cats and is never given to them. Tramadol has weak and variable effect in dogs and is not used as a sole agent.

## Special populations

Neonates, geriatric animals, and those with liver or kidney disease receive reduced doses and longer intervals. Brachycephalic animals receive lower opioid doses and are recovered with an oxygen source and close observation of the airway. Obese animals are dosed on lean body weight.

## Discharge analgesia

Discharge analgesia is matched to the procedure. Soft tissue surgery in a healthy dog goes home with meloxicam for 3 to 5 days. Orthopaedic surgery goes home with meloxicam for 7 to 10 days and a short course of gabapentin. Cats go home with buprenorphine or a short course of meloxicam at the lowest effective dose. Owners are given written dosing instructions, told to give non-steroidal drugs with food, and warned to stop the drug and call if their animal vomits, has black stools or stops eating.

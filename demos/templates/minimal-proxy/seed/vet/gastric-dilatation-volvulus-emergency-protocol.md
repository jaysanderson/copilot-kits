## How gastric dilatation and volvulus is managed

Gastric dilatation and volvulus is a surgical emergency. The sequence is: recognise it, resuscitate for shock, decompress the stomach, take the dog to surgery to reposition the stomach and fix it in place with a gastropexy, then monitor for arrhythmias and complications. Imaging confirms the diagnosis but never delays resuscitation.

## Recognition

The typical patient is a deep-chested large-breed dog presenting with unproductive retching, hypersalivation, a distended tympanic abdomen and restlessness. Signs of shock include pale mucous membranes, a weak pulse, tachycardia and a prolonged capillary refill time. A right lateral abdominal radiograph shows the compartmentalised stomach, called the double bubble or Popeye sign, with the pylorus displaced dorsally to the left of the fundus, which separates volvulus from simple dilatation. Radiographs are taken only after initial stabilisation.

## Resuscitation

Fluid resuscitation with shock boluses of 20 millilitres per kilogram of crystalloid starts before decompression, through two large-bore catheters placed in cephalic veins. Boluses are repeated up to a total of 60 to 90 millilitres per kilogram, guided by heart rate, pulse quality and blood pressure. Hypertonic saline at 4 millilitres per kilogram may be used where volume is limited. Analgesia with a pure mu opioid such as methadone is given early. Blood is taken for packed cell volume, total solids, lactate, glucose, electrolytes and coagulation. Lactate above 6 millimoles per litre at presentation is associated with gastric necrosis and a poorer prognosis, and a fall of more than 50 per cent after resuscitation is a good sign.

## Decompression

Decompression is by orogastric tube first, with trocar decompression only if the tube cannot be passed. The tube is measured from the nose to the last rib, lubricated, and passed gently with the dog sedated. If resistance is met at the cardia, the tube is not forced. Trocar decompression uses a 14 gauge catheter placed in the point of maximal tympany, usually on the right, after clipping and aseptic preparation. Gastric lavage follows tube placement to remove contents. Decompression relieves pressure on the caudal vena cava and improves venous return, but it does not untwist the stomach.

## Surgery

Surgery is performed as soon as the dog is haemodynamically stable, usually within 2 hours of presentation, and always includes a gastropexy. Anaesthesia uses a rapid induction after pre-oxygenation. The stomach is derotated, the spleen is checked for torsion or infarction and removed only if the splenic vessels are thrombosed, and the gastric wall is inspected for necrosis. Necrotic wall is resected and oversewn. An incisional or belt-loop gastropexy is made on the right body wall, because without a gastropexy the recurrence rate in the unit is above 70 per cent, and with it falls below 5 per cent. Prophylactic gastropexy is offered to owners of at-risk breeds, usually at the time of desexing.

## Post-operative monitoring

Ventricular arrhythmia affects around 40 per cent of dogs and peaks 12 to 36 hours after surgery. Continuous electrocardiography runs for at least 48 hours. Lignocaine at 2 milligrams per kilogram intravenously as a bolus, then 50 micrograms per kilogram per minute as an infusion, is started for ventricular tachycardia faster than 160 beats per minute, multiform complexes, R-on-T pattern or poor perfusion. Electrolytes, especially potassium and magnesium, are corrected. Blood pressure is checked every 4 hours.

## Further complications

Other complications are aspiration pneumonia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, peritonitis from gastric perforation, and delayed gastric emptying. Antiemetics and a prokinetic are given after surgery. Water is offered 6 to 12 hours after surgery, and small amounts of a bland diet are started at 12 to 24 hours if there is no vomiting.

## Discharge and prognosis

Dogs are discharged after 3 to 5 days once arrhythmias have resolved for 24 hours and the dog is eating. Survival to discharge is around 85 to 90 per cent in dogs without gastric necrosis and falls to around 50 per cent when resection of the stomach wall is required. Owners are told to feed two or three small meals daily, avoid exercise for an hour after eating, and call the hospital at once if retching recurs.

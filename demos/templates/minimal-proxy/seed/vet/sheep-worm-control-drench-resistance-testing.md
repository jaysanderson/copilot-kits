## Monitoring with faecal egg counts

Worm control starts with a faecal egg count (FEC), not a calendar. Fresh faeces are collected from the rectum of at least 10 sheep per mob, or from the ground in a mob that has just been moved, and delivered chilled to the laboratory within 24 hours. A sample left warm hatches larvae and gives falsely low counts. The result is reported as eggs per gram (epg).

Drenching thresholds depend on the age and class. Weaned lambs at more than 300 epg are drenched in winter and at more than 500 epg in a barber's pole season. Adult ewes are drenched when the mob mean exceeds 400 epg, and sooner before lambing if the ewes are in poor condition. A count below 100 epg in adult sheep needs no treatment. An egg count does not say which worm species is present, so a pooled larval culture is requested when the count exceeds 500 epg to separate barber's pole worm (Haemonchus), black scour worm (Trichostrongylus) and brown stomach worm (Teladorsagia).

## Barber's pole worm

Barber's pole worm is the main cause of death in summer-rainfall sheep. It sucks blood, and 5,000 worms in a lamb can cause fatal anaemia within three weeks. A count above 500 epg in lambs warrants drenching, and in a wet warm season counts are checked every 3 weeks. Anaemia is checked by an eye membrane colour score on a 1 to 5 scale, and sheep scoring 4 or 5 are treated at once regardless of the egg count.

## Testing for resistance: the faecal egg count reduction test

The faecal egg count reduction test (FECRT) is the standard test. It needs at least 15 sheep per treatment group, all with a pre-treatment count above 150 epg. Sheep are weighed, dosed individually to the heaviest in the group, and sampled again 10 to 14 days after drenching. Resistance is declared when the egg count falls by less than 95 per cent after treatment, and suspected when the reduction is between 95 and 99 per cent with a lower confidence limit below 90. A single group is tested for each drench class, plus an untreated control of 15 sheep.

A test costs about A$40 per mob in laboratory fees. The test is run before resistance is assumed, because under-dosing from poor weight estimates mimics resistance.

## Larval development assay

The larval development assay is a laboratory test on eggs recovered from a pooled faecal sample. It needs about 50 grams of fresh faeces from 20 sheep. It returns results within 14 days and is used when many drench groups need testing at once. The assay reports resistance by drench group, and its main advantage is that it does not need sheep to be re-caught.

## Drench groups and rotation

There are four broad drench groups. White drenches (benzimidazoles), clear or yellow drenches (levamisole), the macrocyclic lactones (such as abamectin and moxidectin) and the amino-acetonitrile derivatives. Rotation means changing the group, not the product name. The practice that slows resistance is using two effective actives from different groups together, not in turn. A combination drench is chosen only after an FECRT has confirmed that each component is above 95 per cent effective on the property.

## Refugia

Leaving a proportion of worms untreated slows resistance. Roughly 10 per cent of a mob, the healthiest adults, are left undrenched when the rest are treated, so that susceptible worms remain on the pasture. Refugia are not used for lambs under stress or when barber's pole worm is causing anaemia.

## New arrivals

All introduced sheep are drenched on arrival with a combination of two actives from different chemical groups, then held for 3 days in a yard before release onto a paddock. The quarantine paddock is the one with the least value for later grazing. A second FEC is done 14 days later. Introduced resistant worms are the main way resistance is imported to a property.

## Pasture management

A paddock rested for 3 months in a Mediterranean summer or 6 months in winter sheds most larvae. A paddock grazed by cattle for a season before lambs is safer, since most sheep worms do not complete their cycle in cattle. Lambing ewes go to the lowest-contamination paddock, as the peri-parturient rise in egg output makes ewes the main source for lambs.

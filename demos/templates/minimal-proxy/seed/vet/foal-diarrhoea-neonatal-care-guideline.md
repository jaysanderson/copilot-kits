## Scope

This guideline applies to foals up to three months of age presented to the hospital with loose or watery faeces. It tells the clinician how to decide whether the foal has a self-limiting condition or a life-threatening one, and what to do in the first six hours.

## First assessment

A foal is assessed for demeanour, suckle reflex, temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, mucous membrane colour, capillary refill time, hydration, umbilicus and joints. A bright foal that is sucking normally, with normal temperature and a faecal output that began at 5 to 12 days of age, has foal heat diarrhoea. No treatment is given other than barrier cream on the perineum and daily monitoring. A foal that is dull, has lost the suckle reflex or has a temperature above 39.5 degrees Celsius or below 37.5 degrees Celsius has a systemic illness, and the investigation below begins at once.

## Dehydration assessment

Dehydration is estimated from skin tent, gum moisture, eye position, and capillary refill. A skin tent of up to two seconds and slightly tacky gums suggest 5 to 7 per cent dehydration. Sunken eyes, cool extremities and a skin tent over three seconds suggest 8 to 10 per cent or more. A packed cell volume above 45 per cent with a total protein above 70 g per litre supports the clinical estimate, though the total protein may be low in a foal with failure of passive transfer. Lactate above 4 mmol per litre indicates poor perfusion.

## Intensive care admission criteria

A foal is admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit if it is more than 8 per cent dehydrated, has no suckle reflex, is recumbent, has a lactate above 4 mmol per litre, has blood in the faeces, has a temperature outside the range above, or has signs of sepsis such as injected mucous membranes or swollen joints. Foals that do not meet these criteria are managed in the neonatal ward with twice-daily examination.

## Fluid therapy

Rehydration uses a balanced isotonic polyionic crystalloid by intravenous catheter. The shock bolus is 20 ml per kg over 20 minutes, repeated up to three times if perfusion remains poor. Maintenance fluids are given at 4 to 6 ml per kg per hour, and the dehydration deficit is replaced over 12 to 24 hours. Dextrose at 5 per cent is added when blood glucose falls below 4 mmol per litre. Potassium is supplemented as the foal rehydrates and the serum potassium is checked every eight hours. Milk feeding continues unless the foal has ileus, and the foal is fed 10 to 15 per cent of body weight per day.

## Passive transfer

Serum IgG is measured at 12 to 24 hours of age. A concentration below 400 mg per decilitre indicates failure of passive transfer, and 400 to 800 mg per decilitre is partial failure. Foals under 18 hours old can be given colostrum by mouth if the gut is functioning. Older foals receive hyperimmune plasma by intravenous infusion at 20 ml per kg, with IgG measured again 12 hours after the infusion. A target of more than 800 mg per decilitre is used for a sick foal.

## Diagnosis of the infectious cause

Faeces are tested for rotavirus antigen, Clostridium perfringens type A and C toxin, Clostridioides difficile toxin, Salmonella culture and Cryptosporidium. Blood culture is taken from any foal with systemic signs. Rotavirus is the most common finding in this scenario in foals 5 to 90 days of age and is treated by supportive care alone. Clostridial enterocolitis produces haemorrhagic diarrhoea and rapid deterioration and is treated with metronidazole at 15 mg per kg orally every 8 hours together with intravenous fluids and plasma.

## Antimicrobials and gastric care

Broad-spectrum antimicrobials are started in any foal with systemic signs or a positive blood culture, with a combination of ampicillin and amikacin chosen until culture results arrive. Antimicrobials are not used in a bright foal with mild rotavirus diarrhoea. Gastric acid suppression is given only when gastric ulcer signs are present, because suppression increases the risk of enteric infection. Non-steroidal drugs are used at the lowest dose for the shortest time because the foal's kidney is sensitive to them.

## Isolation and biosecurity

A foal with diarrhoea is moved into the isolation section of the neonatal unit. The handler for the isolation section does not handle any other foal on the same shift, and the diarrhoeic foal is handled last. Staff wear gown, gloves and dedicated boots. The mare may stay with the foal, and the mare is tested for the pathogen. Stalls are disinfected with 1 per cent potassium peroxymonosulfate, with a ten-minute contact time, because rotavirus and Cryptosporidium resist many disinfectants. Salmonella is notifiable to the hospital biosecurity officer within 24 hours of a positive result.

## Discharge

A foal is discharged when it has been bright, drinking, passing formed faeces and free of fluid therapy for 24 hours. The owner receives a written feeding plan and is told to return the foal if the faeces become watery again or if the foal stops sucking.

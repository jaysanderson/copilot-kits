# Anthrax Suspect Response for Livestock and Equine Cases

## Scope

Anthrax, caused by Bacillus anthracis, is a notifiable disease. This procedure applies to any ruminant, horse or pig found dead suddenly, or any animal showing fever, collapse and bleeding from the body openings, in an area where anthrax has not been excluded. It applies to clinicians in the field and to the hospital if an animal is presented.

## The do-not-open rule

A carcase suspected of anthrax is never opened, and necropsy is prohibited. Exposure of the bacterium to air causes the vegetative cells to form spores that persist in soil for decades. The clinician does not cut the carcase, does not collect internal organs and does not move the body. A suspect carcase that has been opened by accident is reported at once to the chief veterinary officer and treated as a contaminated site.

## Recognition

The signs are sudden death with no prior illness, rapid bloat after death, failure of the blood to clot, dark tarry blood from the nostrils, mouth and anus, and the absence of rigor mortis. Livestock may show fever above 41 degrees Celsius, tremor and collapse for up to a few hours before death. Horses show colic, fever and swelling of the throat and neck. A cluster of unexplained deaths over several days on one paddock is treated as anthrax until excluded.

## Notification order

The notification order is fixed.

1. The attending clinician telephones the state chief veterinary officer immediately on suspicion, before any sample leaves the property. The officer issues a submission number and arranges an official veterinarian.
2. The clinician tells the owner to keep all people and animals away from the carcase.
3. The clinician informs the hospital biosecurity officer and, if any person had contact, the public health unit on-call physician.
4. The clinician records the notification time, the officer's name by role, and the submission number in the hospital system.

## Sampling

The only sample taken is a blood smear from a peripheral site, such as an ear vein or a clean cut on the tip of the ear or tail, taken with a needle and not a knife on the body. The smear is air-dried, fixed with methanol and stained with polychrome methylene blue. Capsulated rods in chains with a characteristic ragged capsule are consistent with anthrax. Swab samples are collected from the nostril and double-bagged. Samples are packaged in three layers and delivered to the state reference laboratory by an approved courier, as arranged by the chief veterinary officer. Results of culture take 48 hours and polymerase chain reaction is faster.

## Carcase disposal and site management

Carcases are burned on site or buried at least two metres deep with lime, as directed by the official veterinarian. They are never moved to a rendering plant, knackery or tip. Fuel is placed under the carcase and the burn continues until only ash remains. The ground beneath is dug, treated with 10 per cent formalin or 5 per cent sodium hypochlorite and covered. The site is fenced for at least ten years. Equipment that contacts blood is disinfected with 5 per cent sodium hypochlorite for 30 minutes or autoclaved.

## In-contact animals

All animals in the affected mob or paddock are moved to a clean paddock. They are examined and have temperatures taken twice daily. Animals with a fever receive procaine penicillin or oxytetracycline. Healthy animals are vaccinated with the livestock spore vaccine and kept under surveillance. Restrictions on the property are lifted only after 20 days with no new cases following the last vaccination. Milk and meat from in-contact animals are withheld for the period set by the chief veterinary officer.

## Human exposure

A person who touched the carcase or its blood is treated as exposed. They wash with soap and water, remove contaminated clothing into a sealed bag and seek assessment from the public health unit. A cutaneous exposure is the usual form. The public health unit decides whether preventive antimicrobials are given, usually for seven days in this scenario, and exposed people watch for a painless black-centred skin lesion. Anyone with such a lesion seeks care at once.

## Hospital admission

An animal suspected of anthrax is not admitted to the hospital. The referring clinician is advised to treat on the property under official supervision. If an animal presented unexpectedly is found to be suspect, the vehicle and animal are held in the external bay and the chief veterinary officer is telephoned before any sampling.

## Equine considerations and equipment

Horses are less often affected than ruminants but can die rapidly after eating contaminated feed or being bitten by flies that fed on a carcase. A horse with neck swelling and fever on a property with a history of anthrax is treated as a suspect case, and a clinician examining it wears gloves, a disposable gown, eye protection and a P2 respirator. Vehicles leaving the property are washed at the gate with sodium hypochlorite. Wheel arches and boots are included, and the wash water stays on the property.

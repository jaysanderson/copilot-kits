# Triage Guideline for Western Australian Native Wildlife Admissions

## Purpose

Native wildlife arrives without history, often outside hours, and frequently in the hands of a member of the public who has already been injured handling it. This guideline orders the first thirty minutes of an admission. The first ten minutes are for safety and records, the next ten for a clinical assessment, and the last ten for a release-prospect decision and a treatment plan.

## Handling risk before clinical assessment

For raptors, macropods and large monitors, handling risk is assessed and controlled before any clinical assessment begins. The common injuries in this setting are to people, not to animals, and they occur during an examination that should not have started yet.

Macropods presented in a bag or pillowcase are left in it until the examination space is secured. The room is darkened, the door closed and the number of people limited to two. The animal is examined through the bag where possible, and removed only if the assessment cannot be done otherwise. Macropods are never held upright by the tail or forelimbs. Kicks from the hind legs cause deep lacerations to handlers, and the handler stays behind the legs and holds the animal against the body.

Raptors are foot-restrained first. The talons are the hazard, not the beak, and a towel is placed over the head and body once the feet are controlled. Eye contact is avoided and the room kept quiet. A raptor is not examined by one person.

Monitors are handled by two people or not at all. One person holds the front of the body behind the head and forelimbs, and the second supports the hindquarters and tail. A monitor that has bitten a person is reported to the clinician in charge, because the bite is treated as a contaminated wound, and the person is advised to seek medical review.

## Release prospect at admission

Release prospect is recorded at admission and drives the treatment plan from the first hour. The categories are release likely, release uncertain and release not possible. An animal recorded as release not possible is not placed on a rehabilitation pathway, and euthanasia is the correct first decision rather than a later one.

Injuries that routinely make release impossible include wing fractures involving a joint in flying birds, pelvic injury in macropods, and any injury requiring long-term analgesia in a species that cannot be managed in captivity. A wing fracture involving a joint in a flying bird is therefore recorded as release not possible at admission, and the bird is euthanased on the day.

Other injuries that make release not possible are loss of the upper or lower beak in a bird of prey, bilateral blindness, and a spinal injury with loss of deep pain sensation in a macropod. Absent deep pain sensation is confirmed twice, fifteen minutes apart, by two people before the decision is made.

Release uncertain is used when the injury might heal but the outcome depends on a result not yet available, such as radiographs. An animal in this category has a review time written in the record, and it must be no later than twenty-four hours after admission. If the review does not move the animal to release likely, it is reclassified as release not possible.

## Decision authority

A veterinarian makes the release-not-possible decision. A veterinary nurse or student may recommend it but cannot record it. Where the finder objects, the veterinarian explains the decision in plain language, records that the discussion took place, and does not delay the euthanasia for the finder's preference when the animal is suffering.

## Records

Species, location of find, finder contact and the release-prospect category are recorded before treatment begins. Location of find is required because release is to the point of origin, and an animal without it cannot be released even if it recovers fully. A location is recorded as a street address, a nearest intersection or GPS coordinates. "Perth" or "the river" is not accepted, and the admitting clinician asks again.

If the finder cannot say where the animal was found, the animal is admitted and treated for welfare, but the record states that release to point of origin is not possible, and the release-prospect category is set no higher than release uncertain.

## Pain relief and fluids

Analgesia is given within thirty minutes of admission to every animal in pain, including animals later recorded as release not possible. Fluids are warmed and given by the route suited to the species before any procedure under anaesthesia. An animal in shock is stabilised first and assessed second.

## Hand-over to rehabilitation

An animal recorded release likely or release uncertain leaves triage with a completed admission record, a written plan for the first twenty-four hours, and the name of the carer who will take it. An animal does not go to a carer without all three.

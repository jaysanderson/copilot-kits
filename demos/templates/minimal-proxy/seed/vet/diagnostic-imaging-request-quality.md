# Why Imaging Requests Get Rejected: A Review of Returned Submissions

## Why this audit exists

A returned imaging request costs a day. This audit identifies what causes it, so the teaching can address the causes rather than the symptoms. It covered 214 requests returned without a report out of 3,460 submitted over six months, a return rate of 6.2 per cent.

## The four reasons for rejection

Four reasons accounted for 83 per cent of returns. Absent or inadequate clinical history accounted for 71 returns (33 per cent). No specific clinical question accounted for 48 (22 per cent). Wrong or missing patient identification, including a mismatch between the request and the animal presented, accounted for 36 (17 per cent). Wrong study requested for the question asked accounted for 23 (11 per cent). The remaining 36 were miscellaneous, mostly duplicate requests and unpaid-account holds.

## Absent clinical history

Absent clinical history was the most common single reason a request was returned. An adequate history is four lines: species, age and weight; the presenting problem and its duration; relevant findings from the clinical examination; and what has already been done, including drugs given and previous imaging. A request that says only "lame" or "check chest" is returned. The median cost of this error was 26 hours from request to resubmission.

## No specific question

A request naming no specific question cannot be prioritised against other work. "Radiographs of abdomen" does not say whether the clinician wants to exclude an obstruction, locate a foreign body or stage a mass. The radiologist triages by the urgency of the question: a request that asks "is there a gastrointestinal obstruction in a vomiting dog" is read within the hour, while an unstated question waits its turn in the routine queue, which runs to 24 hours. A good request ends with one sentence beginning "Please assess whether".

## Identification errors

Identification errors fall in two kinds. In 24 returns the patient label did not match the request, and in 12 the laterality or the limb was missing. Laterality errors carry the most risk. The requirement is that the limb or side is written out in words, not abbreviated, and that the radiograph carries a lead marker in the correct position before exposure. A marker added digitally after the exposure is not accepted as proof of side.

## Wrong study for the question

The most frequent mismatch was a request for abdominal radiographs where ultrasound was the right tool, for example suspected pancreatitis or a liver mass, and a request for survey radiographs of the spine where the question was neurological and MRI or CT was needed. The radiologist does not change the study without the clinician's agreement, so the request is returned.

## After hours

Rejections concentrate in after-hours submissions rather than in any particular service. Between 6 pm and 8 am the hospital received 29 per cent of requests but accounted for 58 per cent of returns. Weekend requests were returned at 9.4 per cent against 4.8 per cent on weekdays. No single service, whether small animal medicine, surgery or emergency, exceeded 1.4 times the average return rate once after-hours work was removed.

## Consequences for the patient

Of the returned requests, 31 patients were inpatients whose treatment waited for the report. For those patients the median delay in a diagnosis was 19 hours. Eleven required repeat sedation or anaesthesia because the first study was the wrong study. Repeat sedation is the cost that matters most, because it carries its own risk.

## Changes made

The request form was changed so that the question field cannot be left empty and the history field requires at least 20 words. After-hours requests are checked by the on-call clinician before they are sent. In the three months after the changes the return rate fell from 6.2 to 3.1 per cent, and the median resubmission time fell from 26 to 9 hours.

## A request that works

A request that is accepted on the first pass reads like this: "Three-year-old female desexed Labrador, 28 kilograms, vomiting for two days, painful cranial abdomen, ate a corn cob possibly four days ago, no treatment yet. Right lateral and ventrodorsal abdominal radiographs, with the right side marked. Please assess whether there is a gastrointestinal obstruction." It has species, age, weight, duration, key finding, a possible cause, the views, the side and a single question.

## Urgency categories

Requests are triaged into three bands. Emergency requests, such as suspected obstruction, trauma or respiratory distress, are read within 1 hour. Urgent requests, for inpatients awaiting a treatment decision, are read within 6 hours. Routine requests are read within 24 hours. The band is set from the question stated in the request, not from the service submitting it, and a request without a question defaults to routine.

## Resubmission

A returned request is resubmitted with the missing item added; it does not need a new patient registration. A resubmitted request keeps its original submission time for the purpose of the urgency band, so the clinician is not penalised twice for a clerical return.

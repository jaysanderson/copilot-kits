# Bovine Mastitis and Milk Culture Interpretation

## How to collect the sample

Wash and dry the teats, then scrub each teat end with a cotton swab soaked in 70 per cent alcohol for ten seconds, nearest teat last. Strip and discard the first three streams, then collect about 5 millilitres into a sterile tube, holding the tube nearly horizontal and angled away from the udder. Collect the far teats first and the near teats last, so that the sampler's arm does not drip over a cleaned teat end. Label by cow and quarter, chill at once, and deliver within 24 hours. If delivery will take longer, freeze at minus 15 degrees; freezing reduces the recovery of Staphylococcus aureus by about a third but is better than a warm tube.

Samples are taken before any antimicrobial treatment. A sample taken within 14 days of intramammary treatment gives a false negative in roughly one case in four.

## What the laboratory reports

Culture is on blood agar and a selective medium, read at 24 and 48 hours. Results are reported as organism and a growth category: light (fewer than 5 colonies), moderate (5 to 20) or heavy (more than 20, equal to roughly 2,000 colony-forming units per millilitre). A plate with three or more colony types is reported as contaminated. Contamination is not an infection and is not treated; the sampler is advised to resample.

A no-growth result in a cow with clinical mastitis does not exclude infection. Staphylococcus aureus sheds intermittently, and Mycoplasma will not grow on standard media. A herd with repeated clinical cases, high cell counts and negative cultures is tested for Mycoplasma specifically, by PCR on bulk tank milk.

## Reading the organism

Contagious pathogens (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae, Mycoplasma bovis) spread cow to cow at milking and are controlled by milking order, teat disinfection and culling. Environmental pathogens (Escherichia coli, Streptococcus uberis, Klebsiella) come from bedding, mud and manure and are controlled by cleaner housing and teat preparation. Streptococcus agalactiae is eradicable from a herd, and any positive culture is treated as a herd problem. Coagulase-negative staphylococci in light growth are common skin flora and are usually not treated.

## Chronic versus new infection

A quarter is chronically infected when the same organism is cultured on two samples taken seven to ten days apart. A single positive in a cow with a normal cell count is a probable new or transient infection and is rechecked, not treated. Cows with Staphylococcus aureus in the same quarter on three consecutive monthly samples are recommended for culling, because cure rates for chronic infection are below 30 per cent.

## Cell counts

Individual cow somatic cell count above 200,000 cells per millilitre indicates likely infection. Bulk tank somatic cell counts above 250,000 cells per millilitre trigger a herd investigation: cow-level counts, a culture of all cows above 400,000, review of the milking machine and review of teat disinfection. A bulk tank count above 400,000 puts the supply at risk of penalty under most processor contracts.

## Treatment decisions

Mild clinical mastitis (abnormal milk only) caused by a Gram-positive organism is treated with an intramammary penicillin or first-generation cephalosporin for three consecutive milkings. Gram-negative mild cases are not routinely treated with antimicrobials, since most recover without; supportive care and frequent stripping are used. Severe clinical mastitis, where the cow is systemically ill, receives systemic treatment and fluids at once. The milk withholding period is stated on the product label and is never shortened; milk from treated cows is excluded until a residue-free test result.

## Dry cow therapy

Selective dry cow therapy treats only cows with a cell count above 200,000 in the last test or a clinical case in the lactation. Cows below that threshold receive an internal teat sealant alone. Blanket antimicrobial dry cow therapy is not routine in this hospital's herd advice. A herd moving to selective therapy is expected to keep the dry period infection rate below 10 per cent.

## Teaching case

A herd of 240 Holstein-Friesians recorded a bulk tank count of 310,000. Culture of 31 high-count cows found 14 with Streptococcus uberis, 9 with Staphylococcus aureus and 8 with no growth. The S. uberis cases pointed to a wet calving paddock and were handled by moving calving to a drained paddock, while the 9 S. aureus cows were milked last and rechecked in ten days.

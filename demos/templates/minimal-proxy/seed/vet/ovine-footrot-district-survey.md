# Virulent Footrot in a Southern Grazing District: Survey and Control Outcomes

## Survey context

An unusually wet spring produced conditions favourable to virulent footrot across the district. Footrot spreads when pasture stays moist and ambient temperature is above about 10 degrees, because the causative organism, Dichelobacter nodosus, survives off the animal for only about two weeks on wet pasture and for far less on dry ground. The survey covered 62 sheep properties, 58 of them Merino enterprises, and followed them from first lameness report to the end of the following summer.

## How virulent footrot was confirmed

Clinical scoring alone cannot separate virulent from benign footrot. Properties were scored on a 1 to 5 foot lesion scale, and a swab from the interdigital skin of at least five lame sheep went to the laboratory for a gelatin gel test and PCR for the virulence gene. The gel test needs about seven days; PCR results return in two. A mob was treated as virulent if two or more swabs were positive for the virulent strain. Of the 62 properties, 49 were confirmed virulent and 13 had benign strains only.

## Early inspection

The strongest single predictor of outcome was how quickly the property inspected. Properties inspecting within two weeks of the first lameness report had materially better eradication outcomes: 34 of 38 properties that inspected within two weeks eradicated, compared with 7 of 24 that waited longer. The median delay in the late group was five weeks. Each additional week of delay was associated with about one extra affected mob.

## How the disease was introduced

Shared transport and agistment were the two most frequently identified introduction routes. Of the 49 virulent properties, 17 traced the infection to agistment of sheep on neighbouring country, 14 to shared stock trucks or livestock crates, 8 to purchased sheep without a documented inspection, and 6 to boundary breaches by stray sheep. Four could not be traced. A truck that carried infected sheep and was washed but not dried could carry viable organisms for up to a week.

## What eradication required

Eradication succeeded only where a property committed to a full inspection of every animal. That means every sheep caught, every foot lifted and examined, every lame or suspect sheep culled or isolated. Properties that inspected only the visibly lame sheep missed carriers: carriers showing no clinical lesion made up about 12 per cent of infected sheep in the surveyed mobs. Properties that inspected twice, six weeks apart, in cooler dry weather, had the lowest rate of recurrence.

## Treatment and vaccination

Footbathing in 10 per cent zinc sulphate for five minutes, with sheep standing on a dry hard surface for an hour afterwards, was the standard treatment. It controlled the disease on 15 properties where eradication was not attempted. Antibiotic injection was used on 11 properties for individual valuable sheep, with a withholding period of 28 days for meat. Vaccination reduced lesion severity but did not eradicate infection in any surveyed flock, and was used on 9 properties as a bridge to inspection.

## Farm-level factors separating outcomes

Properties that eradicated had four things in common: they inspected within two weeks, they inspected every sheep, they culled rather than treated chronically affected sheep, and they quarantined purchased sheep for at least three weeks on a separate paddock. Properties with continuous grazing of one wet paddock for more than eight weeks eradicated at half the rate of those that rotated.

## Cost

The median cost of eradication was A$4.10 per head, mostly labour. The median cost of living with endemic disease was A$6.80 per head per year, from lost wool, lower lambing and ongoing treatment. Eradication paid back in the second season.

## Recommendations

Report any outbreak of lameness in more than 2 per cent of a mob to the department. Inspect within two weeks. Inspect every animal. Quarantine introduced sheep for three weeks. Clean and dry trucks and crates, and agist only onto country with a documented footrot status.

## Notification and movement

Virulent footrot is a notifiable condition in the district's disease-control area. A property with confirmed virulent footrot is placed under a quarantine notice and sheep may move only direct to slaughter or under permit after inspection. The notice is lifted after two clear whole-flock inspections at least six weeks apart, the second in dry weather. In the survey the median time under quarantine was 14 weeks for properties that eradicated and more than 40 weeks for those that did not.

## Why mobs relapsed

Of the 21 properties that did not eradicate, 13 had left at least one mob uninspected, 5 had reintroduced infected sheep through agistment after clearing, and 3 had chosen to manage rather than eradicate. Wet weather during the second inspection hid mild lesions and gave a false clear in 4 properties, which is why the second inspection is timed for dry conditions.

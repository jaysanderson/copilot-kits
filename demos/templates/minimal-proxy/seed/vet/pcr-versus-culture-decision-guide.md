# PCR Versus Culture: A Decision Guide for Production Animal Diagnostics

## The short answer

Use culture when the clinician needs to know which antimicrobial will work. Use PCR when speed matters, when the organism is fastidious or slow, or when the sample may have been treated or has deteriorated. Use both when the case is high-stakes, the result will drive a herd decision, or the organism is a notifiable disease.

## Turnaround

PCR returns a result in 1 to 2 working days, while culture takes 2 to 3 days for a negative and up to 7 for slow-growing organisms. Culture of Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis takes 8 to 16 weeks, which makes PCR the practical choice. Susceptibility testing adds a further 1 day to the culture time. A negative culture is reported at 48 hours for common bacteria and at 5 days for anaerobes.

## What culture can tell you

Culture gives a live organism, which can then be tested for susceptibility. Culture is required when antimicrobial susceptibility is needed, because PCR does not give a sensitivity profile. For restricted antimicrobial classes, the hospital stewardship policy requires culture before treatment beyond 48 hours, so culture is the required test in those cases. Culture also detects organisms that nobody suspected, since it is not limited to the targets of a PCR panel.

## What PCR can tell you

PCR detects the genetic material of a named target. It is more sensitive than culture for fastidious organisms. Mycoplasma, Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis and Leptospira are best detected by PCR. PCR also works on samples that were chilled for too long or collected after antimicrobial treatment, since the organism need not be alive. Cryptosporidium, rotavirus, coronavirus and bovine viral diarrhoea virus are routinely identified by PCR.

## What PCR cannot tell you

PCR detects dead as well as live organisms, so a positive result alone does not prove active infection. A positive result in a healthy animal may reflect carriage or contamination. PCR on pooled samples reduces sensitivity, since one positive in a pool of ten is diluted tenfold. Typically a cycle threshold value under 30 is a strong positive, 30 to 36 is weak, and above 36 is reported as not detected. A negative PCR does not exclude infection by organisms not on the panel.

## Sample type and handling

Culture needs a viable organism, so samples are chilled, delivered within 24 hours and put into transport medium if the delay will be longer. PCR tolerates a delay of up to 72 hours when chilled, and swabs can be dry. Do not use a swab with a charcoal medium for PCR as it can inhibit the reaction. Do not freeze a sample for culture of delicate organisms such as Campylobacter.

## Cost

Single-target PCR is about A$45 per sample. A multiplex panel is about A$95. Routine culture with identification is about A$38, and susceptibility testing adds A$28. Cost per case is therefore similar, and the choice is made on information needed.

## Decision by scenario

For mastitis in a clinical cow, use culture. For a suspected Mycoplasma herd, use PCR on bulk milk. For neonatal calf scours, use multiplex PCR plus Salmonella culture. For a sudden death with suspected Clostridium infection, use culture and toxin typing. For abortion in a herd, use PCR panel for Leptospira, Brucella and Neospora, plus culture of fetal stomach content. For suspected Johne's disease in a live animal, use faecal PCR, and confirm negative animals by repeat testing 6 months later.

## Interpreting disagreement

When PCR is positive and culture negative, the likely explanations are prior treatment, a dead organism, or a fastidious organism that did not grow. When culture is positive and PCR negative, the organism is not on the panel, or the sample inhibited the reaction. In both cases the pathologist reviews the result against the clinical picture, and the report states the discordance rather than choosing one.

## How to manage a paralysis tick case

The management of a paralysis tick case follows a fixed order: stabilise the airway and oxygenation, find and remove every tick, give tick antiserum, then monitor in a quiet, cool kennel until the animal can swallow and stand unaided. The sections below set out each step in the order it happens.

## The progression owners miss

Voice change and an altered swallow commonly precede visible limb weakness. Owners almost never report these, because they do not read as neurological signs. They read as a dog being quiet. By the time hindlimb weakness is obvious, the animal has usually been affected for a day or more. The advisory asks practices to put the early signs in their own client communications rather than listing only the late ones.

## Assessment on presentation

The first look is from a distance. Count the resting respiratory rate before touching the animal. Note whether the animal is in sternal or lateral recumbency, whether the bark or meow is muffled, and whether there is lip smacking, drooling or regurgitation. A resting respiratory rate above 40 breaths per minute, any regurgitation, or a failing swallow places the animal in the unstable group and it goes straight to intensive care.

Stable animals have a normal resting rate, can lift the head and can swallow a small amount of water offered on a syringe in the clinic. Unstable animals are given flow-by oxygen immediately and an intravenous catheter is placed in a cephalic vein with the minimum of restraint. Blood is taken for packed cell volume, total solids, glucose and lactate at catheter placement, and a baseline thoracic radiograph is taken once the animal is on oxygen, because aspiration pneumonia is present on arrival in a meaningful minority of cases.

## The tick search

Searching is not prevention, and a negative owner search does not exclude tick paralysis. Ticks are found in the ear canal, between the toes and under the collar, and an owner searching a long-coated dog in poor light will miss one. The hospital search is done properly. The coat is clipped in long-haired animals, and the search covers the ears, the lips and gums, the axillae, the groin, the interdigital spaces, the perianal region and the skin folds. Every tick is removed with a fine-tipped tick remover, using a slow rotating pull close to the skin, and the number removed is recorded. If no tick is found on the first search, a second full search is done after 12 hours, and a full-body clip is used in any animal with a thick coat. Treatment does not wait for the tick to be found.

## Antiserum decision and administration

Tick antiserum is given to every animal with confirmed or strongly suspected tick paralysis and any neurological signs. A single dose of tick antiserum is given slowly intravenously with adrenaline drawn up at the bedside, and a second dose is not given within 24 hours. The dose is diluted 1 to 10 in isotonic saline and run over 30 minutes, with the animal on continuous monitoring. If an acute reaction occurs, characterised by tachycardia, urticaria, vomiting or collapse, the infusion is stopped, adrenaline is given, and fluid support is started. Antiserum is not a rescue for a failing airway, and oxygen, ventilation and nursing still carry the animal through the first 24 to 72 hours.

## Respiratory monitoring

Respiratory rate and effort are recorded every 30 minutes for the first 6 hours, then hourly. Pulse oximetry is used where available, and an oxygen saturation under 94 per cent on room air triggers oxygen supplementation. Animals are kept in a cool, quiet kennel at no more than 24 degrees Celsius, because hyperthermia is common in recumbent animals and tick paralysis impairs heat loss. Intravenous fluids are run at maintenance rates only, because overhydration contributes to pulmonary oedema in affected animals. Food and water are withheld until the swallow is proven.

## What escalates to intensive care

An animal is escalated to intensive care when any of the following occurs: a resting respiratory rate above 40 breaths per minute, regurgitation, oxygen saturation below 94 per cent on room air, a weak or absent cough or gag, or progression to lateral recumbency. Aspiration pneumonia is treated with antibiotics chosen on thoracic radiographs and clinical course.

## Discharge criteria

An animal is discharged when it has a normal swallow, has eaten a small meal without regurgitation, can stand and walk unaided, has a resting respiratory rate under 30, and has been observed for at least 24 hours without deterioration. Most animals meet these criteria 2 to 4 days after antiserum.

## Travel history

Travel history is taken in every suspect case, including animals presented from outside the usual distribution. The presenting location is not the exposure location, and a case that does not fit the local pattern is more often a travelled animal than a misdiagnosis.

## What owners are told

Owners are advised to telephone ahead rather than drive without warning, and to withhold food and water once an altered swallow is suspected. An animal that cannot swallow safely should not be offered water in the car. At discharge, owners are told that the animal remains vulnerable to reinfestation, that a tick preventive is started or continued, that the coat is searched daily through the season, and that the animal is returned at once if its voice changes, it coughs or retches, or its hindlimbs become wobbly again.

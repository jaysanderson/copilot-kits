# Pre-Purchase Examination Standard for Horses

> **Synthetic teaching content.** This document was written for a product demonstration.
> It is not a The university publication, and no case, figure, survey or finding in
> it is real. Do not cite it or act on it clinically.

## Purpose

A pre-purchase examination tells the buyer what the clinician found in a horse on one day, and what those findings mean for the use the buyer has in mind. It is not a guarantee of future soundness. The hospital performs these examinations as a teaching activity, and students observe with the vendor's and the buyer's consent.

## Agreeing the scope

Before the examination starts, the clinician confirms in writing with the buyer the intended use of the horse (for example, low-level dressage, trail riding, or eventing), the agreed price range and the extent of the examination. The buyer chooses between a two-stage examination, a five-stage examination, or a five-stage examination with radiographs. If the buyer declines any stage, the decision is recorded and the certificate states that the stage was not done. The vendor signs a consent form for the examination, blood sampling and any sedation. The clinician does not examine a horse for both parties.

## The five stages

The five stages are done in a fixed order and none is omitted without the buyer's written agreement.

1. Preliminary examination. The clinician checks identity against the microchip and passport, records markings and body condition score, and examines the eyes, heart, lungs, teeth, skin and limbs. The heart is auscultated at rest for a minimum of two minutes and any murmur is graded from one to six.
2. Trot-up. The horse is walked and trotted on a hard, level surface, turned in a tight circle and backed. Flexion tests of each limb are performed for 60 seconds.
3. Exercise. The horse is worked on the lunge or ridden for at least 20 minutes, with the heart rate and respiration recorded at the end. Respiratory noise at canter is recorded. Where a heart murmur is found, an echocardiogram is recommended.
4. Rest. The horse stands in a box for 30 minutes, then the heart and lungs are re-examined, and the horse is checked for stiffness.
5. Second trot-up. The horse is trotted again and compared with the first trot-up. Stiffness appearing after rest is recorded.

## Radiographs

The standard radiographic series is 20 views. It comprises four views of each front foot, two of each fetlock, and four of each hock (in this synthetic standard, two views of each stifle are added at the buyer's request). Radiographs are taken only with the buyer's written consent and the vendor's agreement. The images are read by the examining clinician and a second clinician, and any finding such as an osteochondral fragment, navicular remodelling or a hock lesion is described and graded for relevance to the intended use. Radiographs remain the property of the vendor unless the buyer buys the horse.

## Blood sampling

A blood sample is drawn at the preliminary stage and stored frozen for twelve months. It is tested only if the buyer, within that period, asks for a drug screen, for example if the horse behaves differently after purchase. The sample is labelled with the horse's microchip number and the clinician's initials. The chain of custody is logged on the hospital system and a sample that has been out of freezer storage for more than 30 minutes is discarded.

## What the clinician reports

The examining clinician reports findings and does not pass or fail the horse. The certificate lists each finding, its significance on a three-point scale (insignificant, minor, and significant for the intended use), and any recommended further testing. It does not say that the horse is sound or that it is suitable. The clinician discusses the report with the buyer by telephone or in person before the buyer decides.

## Conflicts and limits

A clinician who has treated the horse, or who has any financial interest in the sale, declines the examination. The hospital will not examine a horse for both parties. If the vendor refuses radiographs or blood sampling, the clinician tells the buyer, and the certificate records the refusal.

## Records and retention

The signed scope agreement, consent forms, findings, radiographs and a copy of the certificate are retained for six years. The buyer receives the certificate within 48 hours of the examination, and radiograph copies are provided on request for a fee set by the hospital.

## Common findings and how they are described

A grade 3 heart murmur at rest is described as significant for the intended use until an echocardiogram is done. A mild splint, an old scar or a small bone spur on a radiograph is usually described as minor for low-level work. Findings in the stifle, hock or navicular region are described against the buyer's intended use, because a horse bought for light trail riding tolerates findings that a horse bought for eventing does not. Wind-sucking, crib-biting and weaving are recorded as behaviours because they affect management and value, even though they are not lameness findings.
